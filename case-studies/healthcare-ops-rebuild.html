Rebuilding a care-agency platform with no original team

US care agencies run their day on this platform: scheduling, staff time, medications, payroll and billing. When it reached us, it was 3 to 4 years old and none of its original developers were left. I won approval for a full rebuild, specified it, and coordinated delivery to production.

01Situation

Care agencies must show, for every client and every visit, that care happened: who attended, when, what was administered and what was observed. They also have to pay staff correctly for that time and bill for it under the right reimbursement model. The platform tied all of this into one record chain: schedule, then clock-in, then medication and notes, then pay, then bill.

The existing build worked, but nobody who wrote it was still around, and there was no reliable documentation of how it behaved. Every change was a risk, and the client wanted to grow the product rather than keep patching it.

The real question

Before anyone could estimate a rebuild, someone had to answer a simpler question: what does the system actually do today, for each role and each module?

02What I did

01 · ASSESS

Understand the legacy

Walked the existing platform role by role and module by module, and wrote down how it actually behaved.

02 · DECIDE

Make the case

Laid out the options and risks for the client, and won approval for a full rebuild instead of more patching.

03 · SPECIFY

Write the SRS

A 15-section SRS covering 7 user roles, 60+ modules, the APIs and the database design.

04 · PLAN

Milestones and sprints

Split the rebuild into milestones and sprints, then assigned tasks and bugs and ran planning and retrospectives.

05 · VALIDATE

UAT and API testing

Designed the mobile app with AI tools, ran UAT and Postman API tests, and demoed every release.

06 · GROW

Recommend features

Turned client pain points and market value into feature proposals the client chose to fund.

Compliance from day one

Because the platform handles protected health information for US agencies, I researched the applicable healthcare regulations before design started and made HIPAA and BAA compliance a requirement, not an afterthought. Access control, audit trails and data handling were specified up front, alongside the functional modules.

A living specification

I kept an SRS tracking worksheet alongside the document. Each module row held its inputs, validations, APIs, data tables, open questions and the client's review status. It became the shared checklist for development, QA and client sign-off, and the input to every change request.

03Key decisions

Rebuild, not patch

With no original developers and undocumented behavior, every patch added risk. A documented rebuild gave the client a platform their team could own and extend.

Compliance is scoped, not retrofitted

HIPAA and BAA obligations were written into the requirements before design, which avoided rework late in delivery.

Grow scope through value, with change control

New features came in through change requests with impact on effort and timeline, so growth never derailed the committed milestones.

05What I learned

  • Document the "as is" before the "to be". On a legacy system the first deliverable is an honest description of current behavior. Without it, nobody can agree on what the rebuild must preserve.
  • A tracker beats a long document. The SRS explains; the tracker gets things done. Teams act on rows with owners and statuses.
  • Scope growth is good news when it is controlled. Recommending features grew the engagement, and change requests kept the delivery predictable.
